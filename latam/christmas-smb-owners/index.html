Across all three

In all three countries the typical small business is one person, often a woman, working from home or a street pitch, who started because there was no job and who pays for everything from her own pocket. Staff are relatives, cash is still the default, and the phone is the main business tool. The numbers differ by country and by source, so this page puts the three side by side and does not average them.

Peru

Portrait in one paragraph

The Peruvian small-business owner is, statistically, a woman in her mid-forties. She runs one of about 6.1 million micro or small businesses, nearly nine in ten of them informal, and in retail (bodegas, boticas, small shops) three in four owners are women. She started out of economic necessity rather than ambition (52.4%), and in about half of cases she heads her household, so the business is the family income rather than a side project. The "staff" are her relatives: 81.4% of MYPE workers are family members and almost all of them are unpaid. Half of these businesses have no dedicated premises and work out of the home, the street or a borrowed space. Only about a quarter keep any accounts, and a retail MYPE earns on average about S/10,700 a year in profit, roughly S/890 a month. Half now hold a bank account, but only one in five has formal credit. In Lima she also lives with extortion and bodega closures (earlier pass). Formality is shrinking: there are fewer formal MYPEs in 2025 than in 2019. The data is strong on structure (gender, age, money, premises) and almost silent on lifestyle, identity, devices and December, which this pass could not reach.

Who they are

  • There are about 6.1 million micro and small businesses in Peru once informal ones are counted. That is 99.7% of all firms, employing 45.2% of the labour force. ComexPerú (ENAHO 2024): about 6.1M MYPEs, +0.6% on 2023, sales S/154,380M (14.2% of GDP, down 0.8%). Source: ComexPerú, Resultados en 2024 (Jul 2025). [well]
  • Informality is the norm and getting worse: formal MYPEs are still about 179,000 below their pre-pandemic level. Informality was 86.8% in 2024 and 86.5% in 2025, against 83.8% in 2019. Formal MYPEs: 801,134 in 2025 vs 980,357 in 2019. Lima is among the least informal regions, but still at 83–85%. Sources: Peru Retail, 17 Sep 2026; ComexPerú 2024. [well]
  • Half of all owners are women, and in retail it is three in four. Women are 51.4% of MYPE owner-employers: 73.6% in commerce (bodegas, pharmacies and other retail), 46.9% in production and 37.3% in services. Source: ComexPerú 2024. [well]
  • The typical owner is in her or his mid-forties, not young. Owners range from 14 to 98 years, with an average of 44: commerce about 46, production about 45, services 43. MYPE workers average 33. Source: ComexPerú 2024. [well]
  • Education is mostly secondary school, with service owners slightly more educated. Commerce owners have mostly completed secondary school, and production owners average completed secondary. Service owners average incomplete technical studies, "significantly higher" than the rest. Source: ComexPerú 2024. [well]
  • In about half of cases the business is the household's main income. 51.6% of owners are heads of household (production 56.9%, commerce 46.3%). ComexPerú reads this as a large share, "if not 100%", of household income depending on the business. Source: ComexPerú 2024. [well]
  • People start businesses out of necessity, and more so now than before the pandemic. Motives in 2024: economic necessity 52.4% (48.8% in 2019), to improve personal or family income 18.8%, to be independent 17.9%, couldn't find a salaried job 4.9%, family tradition 3.2%. Source: ComexPerú 2024. [well]
  • Services are the largest group, commerce is shrinking and production is the oldest. In 2025: services about 3.3M MYPEs (88.3% informal, sales S/75,022M), commerce about 2M (85.6% informal, S/74,694M), production 871,079 (84% informal, S/20,071M). In 2024 the number of commerce MYPEs fell 1.9% while services grew 2.5%. Production MYPEs have the longest average life (10 years) and services the shortest (6.3 years). Sources: Peru Retail, 17 Sep 2026; ComexPerú 2024. Conflict: an Infobae summary of the same 2025 study assigned 3.3M to production and 871,079 to commerce. Peru Retail's attribution is consistent with ComexPerú's 2024 split, so it is used here: Infobae, 16 Sep 2026. [well]
  • Lima holds almost a third of all MYPEs. Lima has 31% of MYPEs, then Piura 7.7% and La Libertad 6.1% (ENAHO 2024, national departments). No district-level split (Lima Norte, Este, Sur, Callao, Gamarra, Mesa Redonda) was found. Source: ComexPerú 2024. [well for Lima total, missing for districts]
  • More than half have no dedicated premises: the business lives at home, on the street or on the move. 47.8% have a space used only for the business (owned, rented or borrowed) and 52.2% have none. ComexPerú's definition of "premises" includes a fixed street stall, a market stand, a shop or workshop, or a room inside the home used only for the business. So 52.2% are below even that bar, working in shared home space, as ambulantes or mobile, or providing services at the customer's site. Of those with premises, 65.1% own, 20.9% rent and 13.9% borrow. In 2025 ComexPerú named the lack of a space to operate as the main brake on formalising (Daniel Najarro). Sources: ComexPerú 2024; Peru Retail, 17 Sep 2026. [well]
  • The basic infrastructure of the premises is poor. Among MYPEs with premises in 2024: public-grid electricity 59.3%, drinking water 22.7%, sewerage 13.2%. The 2025 figures reported by Infobae are 60.4%, 21.3% and 11.1%. Sources: ComexPerú 2024; Infobae, 16 Sep 2026. [well]
  • Not found in this pass: migrant share (Andean internal migrants, Venezuelans), household size, number of children, and owner-level sector detail (bodega, food, Gamarra textiles, beauty, botica, flowers, auto parts, bakeries, online-only). See Gaps.

Archetypes

  • The retail owner (bodeguera, botica, small shop): a woman of about 46 who runs the till with family help and the lowest profit of any sector. Commerce MYPEs: 73.6% women-led, owner about 46, mostly completed secondary, 46.3% head the household, 50.8% have a formal financial product, 34% keep some accounts, 57.9% have a premises. Workers are 88.6% relatives, work 20.9 hours a week on average, and the few paid workers earn S/706 a month. Average annual profit is S/10,717, the lowest of the three sectors. Sources: ComexPerú 2024; closures and extortion in Infobae, 15 Sep 2026 (earlier pass). [well for the national commerce owner; Lima-specific bodega profile only from an unverified Ipsos lead, see Gaps]
  • The producer (workshop, sewing, food-making; the national version of the Gamarra manufacturer): about 45, near gender parity, the longest-running businesses and the family's breadwinner. Production MYPEs: 46.9% women-led, owner about 45, completed secondary, 56.9% head the household (the highest), 52.6% have a formal financial product, only 22.3% keep accounts. The business averages 10 years old, the oldest sector, though 1.3 years younger than in 2019. Production MYPEs employ the highest share of paid workers (12.1%), pay the highest wage (S/894) and have the fewest women workers (44.2%). Source: ComexPerú 2024. [partly: national production MYPEs, no Gamarra-specific data verified]
  • The solo service provider (beauty, repairs, tutoring, independent trades): about 43, more often a man, more educated, and the newest business. Services MYPEs: 37.3% women-led, owner 43, education about incomplete technical, 56.4% have a formal financial product, mostly one-person businesses, an average of 6.3 years old (the shortest), workers about 31 years old, paid workers S/826. Source: ComexPerú 2024. [partly: "services" mixes very different trades]
  • The business without a place: half of all MYPEs, working from home, the street or a borrowed corner. 52.2% have no dedicated premises, and only 13.2% of those with premises have sewerage. The overlap with online selling is plausible but not measured here. Source: ComexPerú 2024. [well for the size; thin for who they are]
  • Not supported by data found in this pass: the Instagram or TikTok seller, the young "emprendedor digital", the Venezuelan entrepreneur and the market-stall owner. Leads are listed under Gaps. [thin]

Lifestyle

  • Small businesses are staffed by the family, and almost nobody in the family gets a wage. 81.4% of MYPE workers are relatives of the owner, and 95.2% of those relatives are unpaid. Only 1.1% of MYPE workers have social insurance. In 2025, 92.1% of MYPE workers lacked a fixed salary (Infobae). MYPE workers average 33 years old and 49.5% are women. Sources: ComexPerú 2024; Infobae, 16 Sep 2026. [well]
  • Working hours are measured for workers, not owners, and they are split between part-time and long days. MYPE workers average 22.6 hours a week, with 19.7% working over 40 hours and 3.9% over 60. Commerce workers average 20.9 hours and production workers 24.8. Owners' own hours, days off and holidays are not in the opened sources. Source: ComexPerú 2024. [partly: owners' hours missing]
  • Fear shapes daily life for Lima shopkeepers (earlier pass, not re-opened). Up to 6,000 bodegas could close in 2026; 15–20% of street-level shops pay cupos; women-run bodegas are hit hardest; SJL, Los Olivos, Independencia and Comas are named. Source: Infobae, 15 Sep 2026. [well, earlier pass]
  • Not found in this pass: commuting and moving goods, devices, social media and WhatsApp use, media, leisure, religion, aspirations for the children, fears beyond crime, and health. See Gaps.

Money and tools

  • Half of owners now bank, almost double 2019, but formal credit reaches only one in five. In 2024, 54.1% of MYPE owners had at least one formal financial product (35.1% in 2019). 52.7% had a savings account, 20.6% a credit product, 3.8% a current account and 0.7% a term deposit. 52.9% used a formal savings method (31.9% in 2019), and 21.7% saved informally. By sector, the share with a formal product: services 56.4%, production 52.6%, commerce 50.8%. ComexPerú links the jump to banking "as an instrument to make sales easier" after the pandemic, which is consistent with QR wallets, though Yape and Plin are not named. Source: ComexPerú 2024. Conflict: Infobae reports that 22.1% "access formal financial products" in 2025. This is probably a different indicator, such as credit, and was not checked against the 2025 report: Infobae, 16 Sep 2026. [well for 2024; conflict for 2025]
  • Most businesses keep no books at all, so pricing and costs are carried in the head. 76.5% keep no accounts, 19.8% keep only personal notes of income and spending, and 3.7% keep accounting books or a system. In commerce, 34% keep some record. In 2025, 22.9% kept accounting records (Infobae). Sources: ComexPerú 2024; Infobae, 16 Sep 2026. [well]
  • Profits are thin, and lowest in retail. Average annual profit for commerce MYPEs is S/10,717, roughly S/893 a month (our division), the lowest of the three sectors. Paid workers earn S/706 in commerce (−24.4% y/y), S/826 in services (−14.2%) and S/894 in production (−6.3%). Total MYPE sales were S/154,380M in 2024 (−0.8%) and S/164,010M in 2025. Sources: ComexPerú 2024; Peru Retail, 17 Sep 2026. [well]
  • Gota-a-gota loans and lack of credit squeeze bodegas (earlier pass, not re-opened). Source: Infobae, 15 Sep 2026. [partly]
  • Not found in this pass: income bands, juntas or panderos, Yape and Plin merchant use, POS, delivery cost and method (own motorizado vs apps), and Christmas campaign loans. See Gaps.

Identity and language

  • The measured self-image is economic, not heroic: most owners say they started from need, and only a minority from a wish for independence. Necessity 52.4%, improving family income 18.8%, independence 17.9%, family tradition 3.2%. Source: ComexPerú 2024. [well as motive; no data on self-descriptive language]
  • Not found in this pass: how owners describe themselves ("emprendedor", "chambeador", "luchador", "mi negocito"), values, Arellano's lifestyle segments and what they say about entrepreneurs, and the critique of the emprendedor myth. See Gaps. [thin]

Colombia

Portrait in one paragraph

Colombia's small business is, overwhelmingly, one person. DANE counts 5.3 million micronegocios in 2024; nine in ten owners work on their own account and 82% of businesses have nobody else working in them. About a third run the business from their own home, and almost one in five sells door to door or delivers to the customer's home. Two in three owners are men overall, but the tienda de barrio is a woman's business: 59% of its 540,000 owners are women, and 62% of tiendas sit inside the owner's house. More owners started because they had no other way to earn (36%) than because they saw an opportunity (31%), and they paid for it themselves: 61% used personal savings, while gota a gota lenders supply almost a quarter of the credit that micronegocios seek, and more than half for street vendors. The business is informal on paper (78% have no RUT, 90% are not in the Cámara de Comercio, 88% of owners pay neither health nor pension contributions) but durable in life: 65% have been running for five years or more. Cash is almost universal (97.5%), but bank transfers have reached 39% of micronegocios and are spreading fast, even among street vendors (5% in 2022, 19% in 2024). Half of micronegocios still do not use the internet for the business, and only 12% of tiendas have any social-media presence. The phone is the shop's main tool: 71% use one for the business.

Who they are

  • Colombia has about 5.3 million micronegocios, and they are mostly a single person: 90.5% of owners are own-account workers and only 9.5% employ anyone; 82.3% of businesses have one person working, 14.9% have two or three, 2.8% have four to nine. Together they employ 6,879,489 people, 77.0% of them the owners themselves. DANE EMICRON 2024 (30 Jul 2025) [well]
  • The urban micronegocio is concentrated in four metro areas: of 2,321,469 micronegocios in the 24 main cities, 25.3% are in Bogotá, 17.5% in Medellín AM, 10.5% in Barranquilla AM and 9.6% in Cali AM (then Bucaramanga AM 5.8%, Cartagena 5.4%, Cúcuta AM 3.4%). Bogotá produces 35.5% of their value added, Medellín AM 19.6%, Cali AM 8.1%, Barranquilla AM 7.4%. DANE EMICRON 2024 [well]
  • Two in three owners are men nationally (64.6% vs 35.4% women), but in towns and cities women hold 38.0% and in rural areas only 29.5%. Women owners grew 1.7% in 2024, men 2.3%. DANE EMICRON 2024 [well]
  • What they do: 23.9% trade and vehicle/motorbike repair, 21.5% farming, 11.9% transport and storage, 10.0% manufacturing, 9.7% arts, recreation and other services (where hairdressers and beauty sit), 8.3% accommodation and food, 5.7% construction. In the 24 cities 58.3% are in services, 29.7% in trade and 11.0% in manufacturing. Trade earns 36.5% of all micronegocio sales. DANE EMICRON 2024 [well]
  • Necessity outweighs opportunity overall: 36.1% started because they had no other income alternative, 31.0% because they saw a market opportunity, 11.6% to practise a trade or profession, 10.8% by family tradition or inheritance, 8.5% to top up household income. Necessity-driven businesses grew 3.7% in 2024. DANE EMICRON 2024 [well]
  • They last: 65.0% have run for five years or more, 35.0% for under five; in towns and cities 39.0% are under five years old. 83.7% were created by the owner alone and 12.6% with family. DANE EMICRON 2024 [well]
  • Formal on paper is the exception: 77.6% have no RUT (70.4% in towns and cities), 89.5% are not registered in the Cámara de Comercio, and micronegocios with a RUT fell 4.1% in 2024 while those without rose 4.1%. 88.4% of owners pay neither health nor pension contributions and 93.6% have no workplace-risk (ARL) cover. DANE EMICRON 2024 [well]
  • The home is the most common workplace: 30.6% operate from the home (32.1% in towns and cities), 17.4% go door to door or to the customer's home, 13.5% on a farm, 12.5% from a shop, workshop or office, 11.3% from a vehicle, 9.6% on the street. Home-based businesses grew 9.8% in 2024 while door-to-door fell 6.5%. DANE EMICRON 2024 [well]
  • Owner age bands, education, household composition, estrato, whether the business is the main household income, and the Venezuelan share of owners were not verified in this pass (EMICRON publishes some of these only in Excel annexes that were not opened). See Gaps. [thin]

Archetypes

  • The tendera de barrio. A woman (58.8% of 540,233 tienda and panadería owners), selling groceries from her own house (62.3% of tiendas are in the home, 36.2% in separate premises), which she usually owns outright (59.9% own and fully paid, 20.7% rented). She runs it alone (72.8% one person) or with family (24.1% have two or three people; 10.4% of everyone working in tiendas are unpaid family members; 19.5% of tiendas were set up with a relative). She chose it more than she was forced into it (48.6% opportunity vs 30.6% no alternative), and she has been there a while (35.2% ten years or more). Almost all take cash (99.4%), 41.3% take transfers, 68.8% use a mobile phone for the shop, only 12.0% are on social media. Tiendas are more formal than the average micronegocio (55.0% have no RUT vs 77.6% overall). DANE tiendas (8 May 2026) [well]
  • The street vendor. Usually a man (60.4% of 507,266 ambulantes), mostly on the move (59.3% mobile, 40.7% fixed spot). Nearly all cash (99.8%), but transfers jumped from 5.2% in 2022 to 19.4% in 2024. When he needs money, more than half go to the gota a gota lender (56.2% of those seeking credit) rather than a bank (30.2%). Women vendors grew fastest in 2024 (+10.9%). DANE EMICRON Vendedores ambulantes (28 Oct 2025) [well]
  • The home-based seller and door-to-door seller. Together almost half of all micronegocios (30.6% at home plus 17.4% door to door or at the customer's home). Home-based is the fastest-growing location (+9.8% in 2024). The data does not say what they sell or whether they sell online. DANE EMICRON 2024 [partly]
  • The owner on wheels. 11.3% of micronegocios work from a vehicle, and transport and storage is 11.9% of all micronegocios (10.7% of sales). This includes mototaxis, independent couriers and drivers; DANE does not split them. DANE EMICRON 2024 [partly]
  • The connected seller (Instagram/TikTok/WhatsApp emprendedor). Data supports only a minority: 49.6% of micronegocios use the internet for the business (61.7% in towns and cities), and businesses using the internet grew 14.7% in 2024; only 12.0% of tiendas have social-media presence. No verified figures on which networks or on online-only sellers were collected. DANE EMICRON 2024 [partly]
  • The Venezuelan entrepreneur. Not verified in this pass. Context carried over: about 2.8 million Venezuelans in Colombia, many saying "ya tengo mi vida acá" ("I already have my life here"); flights from Bogotá to Caracas resumed only on 1 Sep 2026 after the June earthquake in Venezuela. Vanguardia (23 Feb 2026), carried over from the social-context notes [thin]

Lifestyle

  • Family is the staff: 6.0% of everyone working in micronegocios are unpaid family members or workers, 13.5% are paid workers, 3.6% partners; in tiendas the unpaid-family share rises to 10.4%. Rural micronegocios are more often family-built (20.5% set up with a relative). DANE EMICRON 2024; DANE tiendas [well]
  • The phone is the main device: 89.1% use no electronic device (computer, tablet) for the business, but 70.9% use a phone (79.2% in towns and cities, 52.0% rural). DANE EMICRON 2024 [well]

Money and tools

  • They start with their own money: 61.4% of owner-founded micronegocios used personal savings, 15.5% needed no financing, 10.6% borrowed from family, 8.8% got a bank loan and 2.4% went to moneylenders. Tiendas lean more on banks (17.7% bank loans, 60.0% savings, 15.1% family). DANE EMICRON 2024; DANE tiendas [well]
  • Gota a gota is a mainstream lender: of micronegocios that sought credit, 60.0% went to regulated institutions, 22.9% to gota a gota lenders, 13.3% to family and friends; in rural areas gota a gota is the main source (77.0%). For tiendas it is 69.9% regulated, 11.3% gota a gota; for street vendors 56.2% gota a gota, 30.2% regulated. DANE EMICRON 2024; DANE vendors [well]

Status: partial pass, stopped early on instruction. Portrait in one paragraph

The typical Ecuadorian small-business owner runs a one- to five-person consumer business (a shop, food, a service) because there was no adequate job, not because they spotted a gap: 91.5% of early-stage entrepreneurs say they started "to earn a living because jobs are scarce", the highest share in Latin America. Ecuador has the second-highest early-stage entrepreneurship rate of 48 GEM countries (29.63% of adults aged 18–64, behind only Angola), and women are the majority (52.23%). The person starting out is young (18–34 is 55.7% of early-stage activity), has finished secondary school (54.3%), sells mostly to their own neighbourhood but often to other cities too, uses social media more than any other digital tool (66.9%), and in seven cases out of ten has no RUC tax number. Very few make it: only 6.52% of adults run an established business older than 3.5 years, down from 13.26% a year earlier, and 9.09% of adults closed a business in the last 12 months. Crime and extortion became the third reason for closing in 2025 (13.12%). Formal data shows the same churn: Ecuador lost about 101,900 registered microenterprises in 2024 (−9.3%), though INEC warns part of the drop is missing tax data. The survivors skew older (35–44 is the largest group; 55–64 doubles its share) and increasingly frame the business as family tradition (43.11%, up from 37.10%).

Who they are

  • Ecuador is one of the most entrepreneurial countries on earth by share of adults: 29.63% of 18–64-year-olds are starting or running a business under 3.5 years old, 2nd of 48 countries behind Angola (52.90%) and 1st in Latin America, ahead of Chile (29.35%) and Guatemala (25.39%). GEM Ecuador 2025, May 2026 [well]
  • It is overwhelmingly necessity entrepreneurship: 91.50% of early-stage entrepreneurs started "to earn a living because jobs are scarce" (90.55% in 2024), the highest in the region (regional average 82.35%). At the same time 59.23% also want to "generate great wealth" and 51.14% to "make a difference". GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Survival is rare and got worse: established business ownership fell from 13.26% to 6.52% of adults in one year; 9.09% of adults closed a business in the last 12 months (13.51% in 2024, 9.75% in 2023). GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Crime entered the closure reasons: in 2025 "delincuencia y extorsión" was the third reason for closing (13.12%), a cause GEM says had no such weight the year before; lack of profitability fell from 37.40% to 24.07% and personal/family reasons were second (20.34%). GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Women lead the start, men the survival: women are 52.23% of early-stage entrepreneurs (51.18% in 2024) and 54.43% of nascent ones, but 49.20% of established owners; the female TEA rate is 30.38% vs 28.86% for men. GEM calls this the "fuga de género". GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Age: young starters, middle-aged survivors. 18–34 is 55.68% of early-stage activity (18–24 22.36%, 25–34 33.32%) and 53.94% of nascent; among established owners 35–44 leads (27.86%) and 55–64 reaches 15.82%, almost double its early-stage share (8.40%). GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Education: mostly secondary school. Early-stage: secondary complete 54.28%, higher education 23.43%, basic incomplete 13.84%, basic complete 7.95%; among established owners higher education drops to 18.85% and basic incomplete rises to 19.37%. GEM reads this as graduates using a business as a stopgap while job-hunting. GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Not only cities: 43.6% of early-stage entrepreneurs and 38.31% of established owners are rural. GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Sector: consumer businesses dominate and the share is rising. 83.18% of early-stage businesses are consumer-oriented (retail, food, personal services), up from 75.02% in 2023, the highest of the six Latin American GEM countries (Brazil 50.62%, Mexico 69.17%). GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Size: tiny. In the GEM chart on current employment, roughly 77% of early-stage and 70% of established businesses have 1–5 workers, and about 21% and 27% have none; text extraction makes the row labels slightly ambiguous, but GEM's text confirms a "significant" minority of one-person businesses. GEM Ecuador 2025 [partly]
  • Formal vs informal: only 30.6% of early-stage businesses have a RUC (so about 70% are informal) and 55.2% of established ones; 9.2% / 24.86% are registered with the Superintendencia de Compañías; 7.25% / 15.51% are in the popular and solidarity economy register (RUEPS). Among RUC holders, RIMPE is the main regime (60.24% early-stage, 66.96% established). GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Registered universe: INEC counts 1,073,524 enterprises in 2024 (100,461 fewer than 2023, −8.6%); microenterprises are 990.3k (92.2%) and lost about 101.9k (−9.3%), while small firms grew to 61.2k (+1.8%). 489,154 firms (45.6%) are counted via RIMPE. INEC warns the 2024 base is semi-definitive and undercounts because RIMPE data from the SRI was incomplete, so part of the micro drop is a data gap, not closures. INEC REEM 2024, Oct 2025 [well]
  • Churn in the register is huge: 345,821 firms active in 2023 were inactive in 2024 and 245,360 appeared or reappeared. INEC REEM 2024 [well]
  • Sector of registered firms: services 555,374 (51.7%), commerce 365,607 (34.1%), manufacturing 93,814 (8.7%), agriculture 28,113, construction 28,889. INEC REEM 2024 [well]
  • Geography: Pichincha (Quito) has the most registered firms, 297.6k in 2024 (down 26.7k, −8.2%), then Guayas (Guayaquil) 237.7k (243.8k in 2023) and Azuay (Cuenca) 69.2k (76.6k). Firms are counted by tax domicile, so informal Guayaquil commerce is under-represented. INEC REEM 2024 [well]
  • Markets reached: 87.71% of early-stage entrepreneurs sell to customers in their own area, 48.95% to other parts of the country and 13% abroad; established businesses are more local (91.22% / 41.57% / 2.1%). Urban starters: 51.32% reach other cities. GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Tiendas de barrio: the Red Ecuatoriana de Tenderos is reported to count over 130,000 tenderos and about 1.5M direct and indirect jobs, with about 70% of daily household shopping going through the channel and 1,943 shops closing between Aug 2024 and Feb 2025 (11 a day). Search summaries only, pages not opened. El Universo; KCH FM, 27 Mar 2026 [thin]

  • Family tradition is rising as a reason to be in business: 43.11% of early-stage entrepreneurs cite "continuing a family tradition" (37.10% in 2024, regional average 39.08%); GEM calls it "a refuge in legacy models amid uncertainty", and it is the only motive that holds up among established owners. GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Need and ambition sit side by side: 91.5% necessity, yet 59.23% want great wealth and 51.14% want to make a difference. GEM Ecuador 2025 [well]
  • Artisan voice from the monigote trade: Will Tenorio (16 years in the trade) makes figures "de mi misma altura" (my own height) so more people can afford them. El Universo, Dec 2025 [well]
  • Self-descriptions ("emprendedor", "camello", "mi negocito", "el que no arriesga no gana"), values and Costa vs Sierra business culture were not sourced. See Gaps.

Their December

  • Año Viejo monigotes are a December micro-business in Guayaquil: US$10–200 a figure on Calle 6 de Marzo, rising material costs, smaller figures that fit in a car. El Universo, Dec 2025 [well]
  • Yellow underwear and New Year cábalas are a 27–31 Dec trade for informal sellers: US$1 a piece, 3–4 for US$5; sellers expected demand from 27 Dec and hoped the last weekend would beat Christmas sales. El Universo, 27 Dec 2025 [well]
  • December sales share, décimo tercer sueldo-driven demand, extra workload and helpers, seasonal credit, and whether owners work on 24/25 Dec were not reached. See Gaps.

Strongest 8

  • Ecuador has the 2nd-highest early-stage entrepreneurship rate of 48 countries: 29.63% of adults, 1st in Latin America. GEM 2025 [well]
  • 91.5% started because jobs are scarce, the region's highest; necessity, not lifestyle. GEM 2025 [well]
  • Only 6.52% of adults run a business older than 3.5 years (13.26% a year earlier); 9.09% closed one in the last year. GEM 2025 [well]
  • Crime and extortion became the third reason for closing a business in 2025 (13.12%). GEM 2025 [well]
  • Women are 52.23% of early-stage entrepreneurs and 54.43% of nascent ones, but fall to 49.20% of established owners. GEM 2025 [well]
  • 83.18% of new businesses are consumer-facing, up from 75.02% in 2023; four in five sell to households. GEM 2025 [well]
  • About 70% of new businesses have no RUC; 66.9% use social media for the business; 48.95% already sell to other parts of the country. GEM 2025 [well]
  • 92.2% of Ecuador's 1.07M registered firms are microenterprises, and about 101,900 of them dropped off the register in 2024 (−9.3%, partly a data gap). INEC REEM 2024 [well]
